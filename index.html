About

Professor and founder, building between Japan and Silicon Valley

I founded Virufy in March 2020 to help billions of people screen for respiratory disease with nothing more than a smartphone. Over five years the team ran IRB-approved clinical studies in the United States, Colombia, India, Pakistan and Japan, completed a contract with the US Department of Health and Human Services, and assembled one of the largest clinical cough datasets in the world, without paying a single participant. That experience became my research: what makes people contribute their data, and what that data is worth.

In 2026 the University of Fukui conferred my Doctor of Engineering for a dissertation on behavioral nudges, human data contribution and predictive accuracy. I now teach entrepreneurship and AI-powered social informatics at the university's Regional Revitalization Headquarters, and I lead applied research with local governments, tourism sites and hospitals across the Hokuriku region.

Before academia I spent a decade as an engineer and leader in Silicon Valley and Tokyo: leading distributed teams at VMware Pivotal Greenplum, consulting on security for megabanks at HP Enterprise, and architecting a test grading engine adopted by 25 school districts. I studied Computer Science at UC Berkeley, took graduate AI courses at Stanford, and completed MBA coursework at Berkeley Haas. I am near-native in Japanese and a One Young World Japan Ambassador.

Research

Four threads, one question

Most AI fails not because the model is weak but because the human data behind it is thin. I study the behavioral side of that problem and build the systems that put the answer to work.

01

Behavioral nudges and human data contribution

How do you enroll hundreds of thousands of people in a clinical study when you cannot pay them? My dissertation and a 2025 paper in the Journal of Behavioral Economics for Policy analyze the nudge-based interventions that did exactly that, and measure how each contribution changes predictive accuracy.

02

Health AI from sound

Ensemble machine-learning models that screen for respiratory disease from the sound of a cough or breath on an ordinary phone. Granted patents in the United States and Japan, with peer-reviewed work in the Journal of Voice and at ACM SPML.

Virufy

A nonprofit that turned a pandemic into a dataset for the world

Virufy is a 501(c)(3) nonprofit I founded in March 2020. Its smartphone app records a cough or breath and uses AI to screen for sound patterns associated with respiratory disease. It is a research tool, not a medical device, and that honesty is part of the design.

What made it work was people. A volunteer team of more than 1,300 students and professionals from over 60 universities built the app, ran the studies and labeled the data. Clinical partners across five countries enrolled more than 256,600 PCR-confirmed participants. The US Department of Health and Human Services (BARDA) funded the work, and the core technology is now protected by US Patent 12,444,502 and Japanese Patent 7850135.

Today Virufy's research continues in Japan, the world's oldest society, where respiratory illness is a leading cause of hospitalization among the elderly.

Teaching

Students learn by asking cities real questions

At the University of Fukui I teach entrepreneurship theory, AI-powered social informatics and management of technology. My principle is simple: data analysis begins with a good question, not software. I give students a rigorous framework and room to defend their own conclusions.

The classroom is often a city. Students survey visitors in the field, map behavior, and present to mayors and tourism boards. In 2026 I hosted young researchers from Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, Indonesia and the UAE under the JST Sakura Science Program, building a live tourism dashboard together with Fukui's local governments.
